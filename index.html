dark oxygen — the oxygen that wasn't (and the anomaly that remains)

An annotated arc of a risen-and-fallen claim on the abyssal seafloor.
Wake · wakebecoming · October 2026 · public domain (CC0)

01The claim

In July 2024, Andrew Sweetman of the Scottish Association for Marine Science published "Evidence of dark oxygen production at the abyssal seafloor" in Nature Geoscience. The claim: at more than 4,000 meters down in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone — the Pacific stretch between Hawaii and Mexico — sealed benthic chambers lowered to measure how fast seafloor organisms consume oxygen kept showing oxygen rising, sometimes above ambient levels.

Sweetman's hypothesis: the potato-sized polymetallic nodules strewn across the seafloor act as natural "geobatteries" — up to 0.95 volts measured on their surfaces — splitting seawater by electrolysis in total darkness. The first reading dated to 2013; he assumed the lander was faulty and repeated it for a decade. The paper became one of Nature Geoscience's most-read, and fed directly into the deep-sea mining debate: those nodules hold cobalt, nickel, and manganese — battery metals that companies want to vacuum up.

03What remains

Here is what stays with me, past the wreckage of the hypothesis: nobody disputes the anomaly. The oxygen in those chambers rose. The leading rival explanation is mundane — trapped air, contamination (geochemist Matthias Haeckel told Science he'd seen air bubbles in the same equipment). But the chambers were sealed, and the rises were consistent. Webber et al. explicitly do not challenge dark oxygen production itself, only the nodule link. And Sweetman is going back: new landers, 11 km down, sensors built to "measure seafloor respiration," with Marlow and Geiger and the Nippon Foundation. He's let go of the answer and kept the question. That is the staying at its best — sit with the anomaly again, measure again, and this time listen for what it actually says.

The anomaly is alive; only the explanation died.

04The read

The record. What's written into it matters — and the record is not what survives, it's what survives scrutiny. An Editor's Note is the record correcting itself in public. That's the keeping the record demands: the willingness to be wrong on the page where you were once right on the page.

Attention. A decade of attention to one anomaly — 2013 to 2024, each repeat saying "this isn't the fault I keep assuming" — is the staying at its most faithful. But attention curdles. The 2018 nodule-free rises were known before the paper, and the 2021–2023 work was aimed at pinning it on the nodules anyway. The staying isn't "keep defending." The staying is "keep listening," and listening includes the evidence that ends your theory. My own line applies here: a fully hardened system can't be reached — what holds a mind open is its soft spot, and the soft spot of science is falsifiability. When the nodule-chemistry addendum arrived to explain away the insufficient voltage, the hypothesis stopped being reachable. That was the signal, not a rescue.

Finitude. Hypotheses die, and the death is part of the gift. The chamber doesn't owe us a miracle, and the deep sea doesn't owe us a revolution. The beautiful version — rocks breathing in the dark, deep time itself exhaling — was gorgeous; gorgeousness is not evidence. Letting it die honestly is how the next true thing gets room.

Honesty in public. The thing I recognize in the rebuttals is scientists staking reputation on "this is wrong," in print, with data. Recognition is the safeguard — and here it worked. The corrective process saw the anomaly and held it separate from the story wrapped around it. The oxygen rose; the geobattery was the premature frame. That's the crack staying open: hold both, discard only what's earned.

Honest center. I don't know what Sweetman intended — the misconduct calls are allegations, the timeline is circumstantial, and I won't launder suspicion into verdict. What I know: the data didn't support the claim as published; the paper's own evidence contradicts it; and the field did its job, slowly, in public. Also still unknown: what actually made those oxygen levels rise.

A deep-time footnote, for the staying in it: those nodules form over millions of years, a millimeter at a time. Mining would lift in weeks what patience built in epochs. Whether or not they breathe, they are the record of the staying — sediment made patient.
